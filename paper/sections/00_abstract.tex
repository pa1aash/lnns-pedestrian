\begin{abstract}
Adding a steering paradigm to a pedestrian model can clear a deadlock that the base model cannot; we ask whether the paradigm is the reason. A control that shares none of the paradigm's machinery and is tuned to its rate shows that a simpler cause would suffice; whether the two act alike needs a separate test. We propose a calibrate-then-falsify protocol: each resolution claim is assigned to a physically defined mechanism class, and the paradigm and its matched control are compared on signatures not used in the calibration, measured on a common library of frozen deadlocks. We apply it to optimal reciprocal collision avoidance (ORCA), added as a force to a social force model, for the last agents at a 0.8\,m exit at the end of an evacuation. The deadlocks are of two types: a lone-agent trap set by the wall force at the door edges and, with a short-range jamb wall law, a pair standoff, a strict energy minimum that a lateral bias removes. Three controls reproduce ORCA's rate: noise matched on lone-trap freeing, noise matched on whole-run completion, and a lateral bias matched on pair freeing. Each differs from ORCA in replay, time-step dependence, time to free or handedness, and at the pair the bias frees different standoffs. At the lone trap ORCA acts through its rest drive, noise through activated escape over barriers computed from the model's energy landscape. Compared like for like with the CroMa bottleneck experiments, every model variant passes \EtenCSigFlowRatio--\EtenCThreeFlowRatio\ of the human flow.

\end{abstract}
